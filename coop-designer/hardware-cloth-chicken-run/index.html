Hardware cloth for a chicken run

Wall the run in 1/2 in galvanized hardware cloth, 19 gauge, and bury an apron of it along every wall, at least 12 in down or 1 ft down and 1 ft out in an L. Chicken wire keeps hens in but is not a barrier to predators: most can tear through it and rats bite through it (Utah State).

Count the rolls for your run

Hardware cloth or chicken wire

Chicken wire keeps hens in. It does not keep much out: Utah State's extension wildlife specialist says most predators can tear through it and rats bite through the thin wire, and recommends 1/4 to 1/2 in hardware cloth instead. UConn says to secure coops with hardware cloth, not chicken wire, and Colorado State says the same. Florida does say chicken wire gives some protection, and Colorado State finds it fine overhead as a roof against hawks, where nothing can get a grip on it.

Hardware cloth is a welded grid of galvanized steel wire, sold in rolls by its opening and its gauge. Openings matter as much as strength: raccoons reach through openings and grab at the birds inside (Maryland), so UNH asks for openings smaller than 1 in, and the smallest weasels can pass a 1/4 in hole (extension.org).

Colorado State adds that you lose fewer birds behind 1 x 2 in or smaller welded wire than behind 2 x 3 in, and Oregon State builds its runs from 1 by 2 in mesh. Welded wire of that size is stronger than 19 gauge hardware cloth, but its 2 in side is wider than the 1 in UNH asks for, so the tool passes it where there are few or some predators and marks it as one to check where there are many.

The apron against digging

Foxes, dogs and raccoons dig under a run wall; the answer is hardware cloth in the ground along the bottom of every wall. The guides describe three ways:

The tool's default is the L: 12 in down, then the rest of a 36 in roll, 24 in, laid flat under the soil outside, so the apron is cut from whole roll widths and nothing is cut lengthwise. For the six-hen hen house that is 34 ft of apron round the run. A tractor has none: it moves, so its hens are shut in the house at night.

How many rolls

The roll width decides how the walls are cut. The tool tries strips running across and running down each wall, keeps whichever needs less, overlaps each strip on the last by 1 in over a frame member, adds the apron and packs the strips onto rolls. Fixing it to the frame

Staple the cloth to the outside of the frame so a pushing animal presses it against the wood, not off it. The tool's shopping list counts a staple 1/2 in or longer about every 3 in along every member, a washer-head screw about every 12 in round the edge of each piece, and a hog ring or stainless zip tie every 6 in along overlaps that fall between members and where the apron meets the wall. Cut the cloth flush to a wire so no sharp ends stick out at the hens, or at you.

Questions

What gauge hardware cloth for a chicken run?

19 gauge is the usual weight for 1/2 in hardware cloth (Newegg, Do it Best listings); 1/4 in cloth is lighter, at 23 gauge, and keeps out rats and young rodents too. Utah State recommends 1/4 to 1/2 in.

Is chicken wire enough for a chicken run?

Not against predators. Utah State says most can tear through it and rats bite through it; UConn and Colorado State say to use hardware cloth. It is fine as a roof against hawks.

How deep should hardware cloth be buried?

At least 12 in with the foot turned out about 6 in (Penn State, Virginia Tech), 1 ft down and 1 ft out in an L (Colorado State), or 12 in to 1.5 ft in a trench (Maryland).
