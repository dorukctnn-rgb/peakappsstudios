Directive (EU) 2023/970

EU pay transparency, country by country

Every EU country had to give workers the right to ask how their pay compares, and to make employers of every size answer, by 7 June 2026. Malta, Italy and Slovakia have that right in force, Greece from 1 November 2026 and Flanders for its public sector. Elsewhere the laws are still in parliament or we could not confirm them, and Germany still runs its 2017 law.

What changes

Before hiring (Article 5)

Applicants get the starting pay or its range, based on objective, gender-neutral criteria, and the relevant collective agreement terms, for example in the job ad or before the interview. Employers may not ask about pay history, and job ads and titles must be gender-neutral. Check a job ad.

Pay criteria (Article 6)

Workers can easily see the criteria used to set pay, pay levels and pay progression. Countries may exempt employers with fewer than 50 workers from the progression part.

The right to information (Article 7)

Any worker can ask in writing for their own pay level and the average pay levels, by sex, of the category doing the same work or work of equal value. The employer answers within two months at most, reminds all workers of the right every year, and may not stop anyone disclosing their own pay. The yearly notice.

Reporting and joint pay assessment (Articles 9 and 10)

Employers with 100 or more workers report seven pay gap indicators, the first ones by 7 June 2027. Where a category shows a gap of 5% or more that is neither justified on objective, gender-neutral criteria nor remedied within six months, the employer carries out a joint pay assessment with workers' representatives. The reporting rules.

Enforcement (Articles 16, 18 and 23)

Workers can claim full compensation, including back pay. Where an employer has not met its transparency duties, the burden of proving there was no pay discrimination shifts to the employer, and countries set penalties.

For whom

Employers in the public and private sectors, of every size (Article 2(1)), and all workers with an employment contract or relationship (Article 2(2)), including part-time, fixed-term and agency workers (recital 18). Applicants are covered for the rules before hiring (Article 2(3)).

When

  1. 6 June 2023The directive entered into force, twenty days after it was published on 17 May 2023.
  2. 7 June 2026The deadline for national law (Article 34). Each country's own law decides when the rights start there.
  3. 7 June 2027First pay gap reports, on 2026, from employers with 150 or more workers (Article 9(2) and (3)).
  4. 7 June 2031Employers with 100 to 149 workers report for the first time (Article 9(4)).

Questions

Does the directive apply to small employers?

Yes. It applies to employers in the public and private sectors of every size (Article 2). The pay information rules (Articles 5 to 7) cover all of them; countries may exempt employers with fewer than 50 workers from publishing pay progression criteria (Article 6(2)). Pay gap reporting starts at 100 workers (Article 9).

Is the answer always due within two months?

Two months is the longest the directive allows (Article 7(4)), and countries can set less. Malta gives employers eight days. Germany's current Pay Transparency Act, which is not the directive, allows three months.

Can a worker find out what a named colleague earns?

No. The answer gives averages for the women and the men in the worker's category. Where an average would reveal one person's pay, a country can route that figure only to workers' representatives, the labour inspectorate or the equality body (Article 12(3)).

What does 'not confirmed' mean in the table?

We could not confirm on an official source whether that country has put the right to information into force. We list what EUR-Lex shows the country has notified and do not guess. The request letter for those countries cites the directive and claims no national rule.

How often is the table checked?

Each row carries its sources, and the whole table was checked on 8 October 2026. EUR-Lex updates its list of national measures weekly; national parliaments publish their bills and acts as they pass.
