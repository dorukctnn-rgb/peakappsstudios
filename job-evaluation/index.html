Three steps

  1. Add the roles you want to compare, by hand or from a CSV or the Excel template.
  2. Score each role on fourteen subfactors, reading what each level means. The points add up as you go.
  3. Read the grades. Roles in the same grade are work of equal value, and the bias checks say what to look at again.

            How the points work

            The plan. Four factors and fourteen subfactors, as in the EU-wide guidelines on gender-neutral job evaluation published by the European Commission and the European Institute for Gender Equality in March 2026. By default skills carry 40% of the points, responsibility 35%, effort 15% and working conditions 10%, out of 1,200.

            Levels to points. Each subfactor has levels from 0 to 5, and knowledge from 0 to 8, each with a description of what the job requires. Its points are spread evenly over its levels: communication carries 96 points, so each level adds 19.2. A role’s total is the sum over the fourteen subfactors. A worked example.

            Grades and categories. Ten grades of 120 points, as in the guidelines’ workbook: grade 1 runs from 0 to 120 points, grade 2 from 121 to 240, and so on. The roles in one grade are treated as work of equal value, a category of workers in the sense of Article 3(1)(h) of the directive.

            The bias checks. Weighting that leans on one factor or leaves a subfactor out; subfactors that give every role the same level; a women-dominated role a grade below a men-dominated role it matches on three factor groups; and the ILO guide’s test of whether the subfactors where women-dominated roles score higher weigh less. Roles count as women-dominated at 60% women or more, as in the guidelines’ workbook.

            What it does not do for you

            It certifies nothing. The directive approves no method, and nor do we. This is a point-factor method designed to be gender-neutral, following the guidelines and the ILO guide. The guidelines say themselves that using them does not certify or guarantee compliance.

            Workers’ representatives. Article 4(4) wants the criteria agreed with workers’ representatives where they exist, and national law may give them a formal say: the works council’s consent to a job evaluation system in the Netherlands, co-determination of pay structures in Germany. Involve them before the scores are final.

            Review and record. Score the job, not the person in it. Review the results for bias, and write down every decision and its reason. The record is the evidence behind an answer to a pay information request (Article 7), the category gaps in a pay report (Article 9(1)(g)) and a joint pay assessment (Article 10); in a dispute the burden of proof can shift to the employer (Article 18).

            Questions

            Is this a certified or approved job evaluation method?

            No. The directive does not certify or approve any method, and neither do we. This is a point-factor method designed to be gender-neutral, built on the factor plan of the EU-wide guidelines and the ILO guide. The guidelines themselves say that using them does not certify or guarantee compliance; that depends on how the evaluation is carried out.

            Where do the factors, weights and levels come from?

            From the EU-wide guidelines on gender-neutral job evaluation and classification, published by the European Commission and the European Institute for Gender Equality (EIGE) in March 2026: four factors, fourteen subfactors, default weights on 1,200 points and ten grades of 120 points. The guidelines call the weights illustrative. The level descriptions here are shortened from theirs.

            Which roles should I evaluate?

            Each distinct job, not each person: people doing the same work share one role. Rate what the job requires, not the qualifications or performance of whoever holds it. Use the job titles from your payroll if you want to add the categories to a payroll export later.

            What is a category of workers?

            Article 3(1)(h) of Directive (EU) 2023/970 defines it as workers performing the same work or work of equal value, grouped in a non-arbitrary way on objective, gender-neutral criteria. Here, the roles whose points fall in the same grade form one category, as the guidelines suggest. The categories decide which averages a worker receives on request (Article 7) and the gaps reported under Article 9(1)(g). Work of equal value, with examples.

            Do workers’ representatives have to be involved?

            Article 4(4) says the criteria are agreed with workers’ representatives where such representatives exist, and Article 3(1)(h) mentions their cooperation in grouping workers in accordance with national law. National law may also give them a formal say: in the Netherlands the works council must consent to a job evaluation system, and in Germany the works council co-determines pay structures. Involve them before the scores are final.

            Is anything uploaded?

            No. The roles, scores and any payroll file you merge are processed by this page in your browser. Nothing is sent to a server and closing the tab discards it, unless you save the evaluation file to your own computer. With Pro, only the licence key is sent to Gumroad to check it.

            Does it work for equal value claims in the UK?

            The Equality Act 2010 treats work as equal when it is rated as equivalent by a job evaluation study (section 65(4)), and a tribunal must accept a study’s different values unless it suspects the study discriminates because of sex or is otherwise unreliable (section 131(6)). Whether an evaluation counts as such a study depends on how it was carried out, so the record of the scheme, the scores and the reasons matters.
