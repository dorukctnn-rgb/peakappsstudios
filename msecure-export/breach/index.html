mSecure breach: what to change first

On November 3, 2026 mSeven, the maker of mSecure, e-mailed its users about suspicious activity and asked them to protect everything stored in the app. The notice did not say what was taken. Here is what was reported, and an order that gets the most important accounts done first.

Checked November 5, 2026

What is known (November 5, 2026)

  • November 3, 2026: mSeven e-mails mSecure customers about suspicious activity associated with the mSecure password management application and asks them to immediately take steps to protect all confidential information stored in it (Malwarebytes).
  • Suggested steps: changing passwords, resetting or re-enrolling two-factor authentication methods, or removing existing passkeys (BleepingComputer).
  • Not said: which systems, whether customer data was read, how many users. BleepingComputer had no answer from mSeven when it published on November 4.
  • From support replies shared by customers (not an official page): an encrypted database was taken from mSecure Cloud; only users syncing with mSecure Cloud are affected.

The order to change logins in

  1. E-mail and account recovery. Your main inbox can reset nearly every other password. Change it, check its recovery phone and address, and sign out of other sessions.
  2. Money. Banks, cards, PayPal and other payment apps, investing, tax.
  3. Phone and the big accounts. Your carrier (SIM swaps start there), Apple, Google, Microsoft, Amazon.
  4. Work and files. Work tools, code hosting, cloud storage.
  5. Social and messages. Accounts that speak in your name.
  6. Everything else.

Inside each group, go first to any password you used on more than one login: one leaked copy opens all of them. Where a login had two-factor set up with a secret stored in mSecure, turn two-factor off and on again so a new secret replaces the old one, and remove passkeys that were kept in mSecure.

Change pages that open in one click

Many sites answer the address https://site/.well-known/change-password with their own change-password page, a W3C convention password managers use. Rekey links every login to that address and to the site’s home page, for the sites that do not support it.

Before you delete mSecure

Export first: the CSV export is in mSecure for Mac and Windows (steps). The file is plain text, so import it into the new manager, keep it only as long as you need it, then delete it and empty the trash. Moving to Apple Passwords, Bitwarden or 1Password does not change any password by itself.

Questions

What did mSecure say?

Its notice of November 3, 2026, quoted by BleepingComputer and Malwarebytes, says mSeven "recently learned of suspicious activity associated with the mSecure password management application" and that steps "may include changing passwords, resetting or re-enrolling two-factor authentication methods, or removing existing passkeys". It does not say what systems were involved or whether vault data was read.

Was my vault stolen?

Not known from the notice. BleepingComputer reported support replies, shared by customers, saying an encrypted database was taken from mSecure Cloud and that the issue only concerns users who sync with mSecure Cloud. Those replies are not an official incident page; treat every stored password as possibly exposed until mSecure says more.

Which passwords first?

Your main e-mail and anything that can reset other accounts, then banks, cards and payments, then your phone carrier and Apple, Google, Microsoft and Amazon accounts, then work. Inside each group, first any password you used on more than one site. Malwarebytes gives the same priority: primary e-mail, financial, work and account-recovery credentials.

Should I change my mSecure master password?

mSecure’s own help page on an unauthorized sign-in says to change your most sensitive passwords first, then every active account, and describes resetting the mSecure account, which deletes all data in it. Export your data before any reset.

Does Rekey know if an account was breached?

No. It looks at your file only: it groups logins by kind, finds passwords used more than once and marks logins with two-factor secrets. It does not look anything up online.
