      How the figures are worked out

      Hourly pay. In the UK it is ordinary pay plus any bonus paid in the pay period that includes the snapshot date, turned into a weekly amount (multiplied by 7 over the days in the pay period, with a month counted as 30.44 days) and divided by weekly working hours. In Ireland it is ordinary pay plus bonus over the 12 months to the snapshot date, divided by the hours worked in those 12 months; Irish ordinary pay includes overtime, UK ordinary pay does not. For the EU directive it is gross pay for the year, basic and complementary or variable, over the hours paid.

      The gap. Every gap is (A minus B) divided by A, times 100, where A is the figure for men and B the figure for women. A positive gap means men are paid more; a negative gap means women are. Mean is the average; median is the middle value, or the average of the two middle values when the count is even.

      Bonus. Bonus gaps compare the mean and median bonus of the men and women who were paid one in the 12 months to the snapshot date. The share paid a bonus divides by all men or all women, including those not paid one.

      Quartiles, rounding and people left out

      Quartile pay bands. Everyone with hourly pay is ranked from lowest to highest and split into four bands as equal as possible. As the GOV.UK guidance sets out, one person left over goes to the lower band, two go to the lower and upper middle bands, three to the lower, lower middle and upper middle. Where people on the same hourly pay fall either side of a dividing line, each band gets the same mix of men and women.

      Rounding. Figures are calculated unrounded and shown to one decimal place. An exact half goes to the even digit, so the two shares in each band still add up to 100.0.

      Who is left out. In the UK, employees on reduced or nil pay because of leave are left out of the hourly figures and kept in the bonus figures. Rows without a usable sex, pay or hours are listed in the checks before any figure, and Pro writes them to an audit file.

      Sources: SI 2017/172; SI 2017/353, Schedule 1; GOV.UK, Making your calculations (updated 21 May 2026); S.I. No. 264 of 2022 as amended by S.I. No. 259 of 2024 and S.I. No. 212 of 2025; Directive (EU) 2023/970. Last checked 8 October 2026.

      Questions

      Is my payroll file uploaded anywhere?

      No. The file is read by JavaScript in this page, on your computer, and the figures are worked out there. Nothing about the file or its contents is sent to a server, and closing the tab discards it. If you buy Pro, only the licence key is sent to Gumroad to check it.

      Which employers have to report?

      In Great Britain, employers with 250 or more employees on the snapshot date: 5 April for private and voluntary employers, 31 March for most public authorities. In Ireland, employers with 50 or more employees on a snapshot date they choose in June. Under the EU Pay Transparency Directive, employers with 100 or more workers, starting with those of 150 or more by 7 June 2027. The UK, Ireland and EU directive guides have the details.

      What does the file need to contain?

      One row per employee on the snapshot date, with their sex and either their hourly pay or their pay and hours for the period, plus bonus pay for the bonus figures. Ireland adds part-time, temporary contract and benefits in kind columns; the EU directive adds a category of workers. The CSV template lists every column.

      How are employees who are not recorded as male or female handled?

      They are counted and shown, and left out of the calculations. For the UK, the GOV.UK guidance updated on 21 May 2026 says reporting must be based on each employee’s biological sex, so correct those records before you publish. For Ireland, the Department’s FAQ says an employee who does not identify as male or female may be omitted from the calculations. The directive does not address it.

      Why might my figures differ from my payroll software?

      Usually because of what went into the file: which pay counts as ordinary pay, who is a full-pay relevant employee, which bonuses fall in the 12 months, or how weekly hours were set. The tool shows how each figure was worked out, lists every row it left out and why, and rounds only at the end.

      Is this legal advice?

      No. It calculates the measures from the data you give it, using the definitions in the Regulations and the directive. You still decide who is a relevant employee, what counts as ordinary pay and bonus pay, and what your report says. The employer is responsible for the accuracy of the published figures.
